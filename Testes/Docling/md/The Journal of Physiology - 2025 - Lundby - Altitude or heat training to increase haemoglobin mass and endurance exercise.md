Abstract High-altitude training has been a well-integrated tool in elite endurance sport for decades. The underlying premise is that physiological responses to high-altitude exposure, such as an increase in haemoglobin mass, are correlated with exercise performance. In this review, we highlight that physiological adaptations to a given magnitude of hypoxic exposure induce a highly variable range of responses in humans. For haemoglobin mass, this can vary from near nothing to previously unseen augmentations. At present, however, it remains inconclusive whether responses observed after one high-altitude training camp can be reproduced at the individual level during a subsequent camp. Exercise training performed in a warm environment or by wearing heat-capturing garments in a cooler environment has been termed 'heat training' and has, in recent years, become an increasingly popular alternative to altitude training. After a 3- to 5-week period of heat training, a robust increase in haemoglobin mass has repeatedly been reported, although the associated performance gains are only small. Accordingly, heat training should probably not be implemented before all other steps involved in elite training are in place and, importantly, heat training should not be performed at the expense of other important aspects of elite life. Thus, heat training might serve as a substitute for altitude training. Ultimately, it can be recommended that elite athletes experiment with either approach and that the choice of implementation should be made at the individual level, ensuring that the applied stress (hypoxia or heat) is tolerated adequately by the athlete.

## Introduction

Altitude and normobaric, hypoxic training/exposure in any of its forms has been and continues to be an important pillar for many endurance athletes/sport federations in their preparation for big sport events. In a similar manner, the implementation of heat training into the training regimes of athletes seems to have received much attention in recent years, and therefore these two strategies are the focus of this review.

It should be clear that when aiming to compete at high altitude, which for athletes might begin as low as 600 m a.s.l., or in environments with higher ambient temperatures than in one's home setting, acclimatization to those conditions, or training in similar environments while residing at home, will very probably improve exercise performance in those specific environments. The aim of this review, however, is not to discuss such an approach, but to outline the use of altitude exposure (i.e. live high-train high and live high-train

It should also be emphasized that an exercise performance outcome might provide a ' competitive significance' long before it reaches 'statistical significance' in a scientific study. Furthermore, it should be acknowledged that it is unlikely that laboratory measures of, for example, maximal oxygen uptake ( ˙ V O2max) and similar tests, are of great relevance for evaluating real-life outdoor exercise performance, such as marathon running or bicycling races. Finally, it should also be acknowledged that in most scientific contexts, where study means are being evaluated and reported, within those means both positive and negative values are typically present. ## Altitude training

Nearly 10 years have past since our last review on altitude training (Lundby &amp; Robach, 2016). The main conclusion stated back then was that 'Based on the available literature, the foundation to recommend altitude training to athletes is weak.' . Reasons for this conclusion included that the majority of studies had been conducted without proper control groups, with inclusion of only small subject numbers and not performed in elite athletes. In addition, although markers for endurance exercise performance, such as ˙ V O2max, haemoglobin mass (Hbmass) and lactate thresholds, have typically been determined, true exercise performance was very seldomly evaluated, and reported effect sizes were typically small. A few years later and fully in agreement herewith, Bejder and Nordsborg (2018) concluded that 'Importantly, when control groups, blinding procedures, and strict scientific evaluation criteria are applied, LHTL [live high-train low] has no detectable effect on performance.' , In contrast, others (Garvican et al., 2012; Wehrlin et al., 2016) have earlier expressed the opinion that negative studies might be related to the use of different methodology, although these also have been counter-argued (Lundby et al., 2012), amongst others by collaboration between both 'sides' (Keiser et al., 2017). Nonetheless, despite the pros and cons, one might ask whether the field has advanced since our last review on this topic.

Mirroring the past, a good number of studies have been published within the last decade that do not include a sea-level control group, do not control or even report exercise training volume/intensity, or any other sort of control for that matter. Although such observational analysis might provide sport federations and teams with information on whether a given altitude strategy might improve certain outcomes, it should also be clear that from a scientific point of view, such opportunistic research has only limited value. Also, it needs to be acknowledged that there is a clear difference between studies observing participation of national athletes in high-altitude training camps, where participating scientists will have only limited control over the duration, training, diet etc., and, at the other end of the spectrum, purposefully conducted scientific studies with rigorous control of external inputs, but probably not performed by top-calibre athletes. In some cases, it is not entirely clear whether a publication is based on a purposefully designed study protocol or whether scientists have taken the opportunity to observe ongoing activities or, perhaps, even a combination of the two.

Much focus has been given to high-altitude training and its effects on Hbmass, for the simple reason that a high Hbmass is an important factor for endurance-exercise performance, is known to be influenceable by hypoxia and is relatively easy to measure. Before delving into altitude training and Hbmass data, it should also be stated clearly that Hbmass is only one component of the highly complex nature of exercise performance, which is, in our opinion, very superficially understood. In some studies, Hbmass might increase but might then not reveal the expected increase in ˙ V O2max or performance tests. Authors might then rightfully speculate that high-altitude training might facilitate haematological but not performance parameters. Our take on this, however, is that the continuous fine-tuning of carbon monoxide (CO) rebreathing methodology conducted over the last 20 years to determine Hbmass allows for highly precise assessment of Hbmass, if performed with the greatest of care, and also with the introduction of repeated measurements within a single measurement session (Lundby et al., 2023), whereas the variation for exercise performance tests is usually considerably larger, and thus requires greater statistical power/number of study participants to manifest into 'significant' differences. This might also highlight, however, that potential performance benefits of altitude training might not be substantial.

Recent studies on altitude training. A total of 31 studies published in the last decade are summarized in Table 1. Among the 19 controlled studies, 12 examined changes in Hbmass, with seven (67%) reporting a positive effect of altitude training. Twelve studies evaluated changes in ˙ V O2max, of which four (33%) reported improvements. Performance outcomes were assessed in 17 studies, with 8 (47%) demonstrating improvements following altitude exposure. It is worth noting that training load data are absent in 21% of the controlled studies (and 42% of the uncontrolled studies), limiting the ability to exclude confounding effects of training. Among the 12 uncontrolled studies, all 10 that assessed Hbmass reported a positive response to altitude training. One of the four studies assessing ˙ V O2max found a positive effect, and three of the seven studies evaluating performance outcomes reported improvements (based on competition results in two of the three studies).

In summary, recent studies reveal substantial individual variability in responses to altitude training, with a majority indicating slight Hbmass expansion, but fewer than half reporting performance benefits. Furthermore, the studies highlight the recurring challenge of monitoring and/or controlling training loads during training camps, which often hinders the drawing of robust conclusions about the real effects of altitude training.

Accordingly, Levine's group has reported considerable variation in the erythropoietin (EPO) response to acute hypoxic exposure (Ge et al., 2002). Regardless, the variation in Hbmass response to high-altitude training has been the target for investigation by two research groups, in particular, who have assessed data gathered during national team camps (Nummela et al., 2021; Skattebo &amp; Hallen, 2022). In these studies, however, sea-level control training is lacking, and it cannot be ruled out that the variations observed would have been similar if studied at sea level. The Hbmass is not a steady value, with an average daily turnover of erythrocytes of ∼ 1%, and this number can obviously vary depending on many factors.

Nummela et al. (2021) set out to determine whether athletes can be classified as responders or non-responders. The total dataset included data from an impressive 82 high-altitude training camps and with the inclusion of 59 athletes. The degree of altitude ranged from 1350 to 2500 m. The data revealed that Hbmass was higher in 56% of all participants in comparison to pre-altitude training (but given that there was no control group, not in comparison to sea-level control training) and that this number was increased to 65% when only camps conducted above 2000 m a.s.l. were considered. In a subgroup of 15 athletes participating at least twice in camps, they found that 27% always had a positive Hbmass response following a high-altitude training camp, whereas 13% always a negative response and 60% had both a positive and a negative response. Accordingly, the authors concluded that this 'does not support classification of an athlete permanently as a responder or non-responder' , but nonetheless support the responder vs . non-responder concept as such, but which, in a strict sense, could also be the result of external/ biological factors, such as fluctuations over time. Intriguingly, Robertson et al. (2010) demonstrated some 15 years ago that although group mean response could be replicated for Hbmass between two identical high-altitude training camps, this was not the case at the individual level. Of note, exercise performance in that study could not be reproduced even at the group mean level.

Realizing that Hbmass fluctuates across a sport season, Skattebo and Hallen (2022) tested whether a low individual baseline measure of Hbmass would lead to a greater response in Hbmass when national team athletes participated in a high-altitude training camp (i.e. observational study with no control group). They did so by calculating the individual mean baseline for Hbmass of three to five high-altitude training camps, then investigated whether the percentage deviation from that individual mean baseline affected the altitude-induced Hbmass response. The conclusion from the study is that an initially low Hbmass before sojourn affects the high-altitude response in a positive manner. Whether this is caused by hypoxia as such or whether it exemplifies the natural variation reported, being 3-5% over 10-12 months (Garvican et al., 2010; Prommer et al., 2008), remains uncertain. Of note, Peltonen et al. (2024) did not find an association between initial sea-level baseline values and the response at high altitude. However, given that they also included the sea-level group Hbmass data in their calculation, the validity of their observation is questionable. Nevertheless, although we and others have previously suggested an association between the initial level of Hbmass and its potential expansion with high-altitude training (Koivisto-Mørk et al., 2021; McLean et al., 2013; Robach &amp; Lundby, 2012; Skattebo &amp; Hallen, 2022), the existence of a 'physiological ceiling' for Hbmass remains difficult to explain and is not always confirmed. Observational data (without a sea-level control group) indicate that even elite cyclists in the competition period can slightly increase their Hbmass following altitude training (Cubel et al., 2025).

In the same study population as used by Skattebo and Hallen (2022), Koivisto-Mørk et al. (2021) analysed whether baseline values for serum ferritin in athletes with clinically normal iron stores and iron supplementation during the camp had an effect on the Hbmass response. Noassociations were found, however. In a similar manner, Nummela et al. (2021) found that the Hbmass response to high-altitude training above 2000 m increased from 65 to 69% when only those with normal ferritin levels where included, suggesting a small negative effect if ferritin levels are lower than normal.

Although responder vs . non-responder seems mostly to be discussed in relationship to athletes participating in altitude training, one might ask whether this phenomenon is common also among healthy humans exposed to high altitude? When exposing eight healthy non-sport-active individuals to 3450 m altitude for 1 month, we observed Hbmass to increase on average by 5.3%, but also that the magnitude of increase ranged from 2.5 to 11.1%, i.e. from what is barely detectable with the CO rebreathing method to a 'sizeable' increase (Siebenmann et al., 2015).

The high-altitude responder/non-responder phenomenon might perhaps not be all that different from human adaptations to other external stimuli. One such example could be exercise training, where a given training dose can cause marked different increases in exercise performance. In one such study, ˙ V O2max is reported to increase by 3-60%, although training was identical among study participants (Montero &amp; Lundby, 2017). In that study, participants who trained less frequently per week, possibly reflecting an insufficient 'training dose' comparable to 'too' low an altitude dose, included some non-responders to exercise training, whereas no non-responders were found among those training more than four times per week, a pattern that might be comparable to the effects of higher altitude exposure or longer exposure duration. It is perhaps easy to imagine that non-responders to a given degree of hypoxia might respond if the dose and/or degree is increased, although

this remains unexplored.

The obvious consequence of this would be to identify altitude responders (to altitudes where athletes can still live and train), then prolong the stimulus for as long as deemed possible, but at the same time without negatively affecting other parts of athletes' lives.

Attempts to prolong the hypoxic effect on Hbmass. A consistent observation is that Hbmass decreases back to baseline values within 1-2 weeks following high-altitude exposure (Klein et al., 2021; Siebenmann et al., 2015), which is in stark contrast to the expected lifespan of 120 days of a red blood cell. Based on animal experiments, an initial explanation given for this phenomenon was a selective destruction of the newest synthesized blood cells, accordingly termed 'neocytolysis' (Song et al., 2017). More recently, in humans it was demonstrated that erythropoiesis was essentially blocked upon return to sea level (Klein et al., 2021), which would decrease Hbmass by ∼ 1%/day if terminated completely (theoretically meaning ∼ 50 g Hbmass in a week in a person with an Hbmass of 750 g). It could thus be speculated that an intervention/stimulus facilitating circulating EPO upon return to sea level could limit or entirely abolish the loss of Hbmass upon return to sea level. Two strategies have been exploited thus far, with positive outcomes. First, Peltonen's research team found that in 22 national team athletes (from mixed disciplines) performing (unsupervised) intermittent hypoxic exposure (2 h rest in normobaric hypoxia) and hypoxic exercise (1 h at low intensity) every third day for 1 month following 1 month of high-altitude training (various protocols), the elevated Hbmass was maintained, whereas this was not the case for the 22 athletes in the control group who simply returned to sea level, with no attempts made to preserve the elevated Hbmass. Second, in a similar manner, Rønnestad et al. (2025) showed that three weekly heat-training sessions (see below) performed over 3.5 weeks is an effective strategy for maintaining the elevated Hbmass in a mix of under-23 national team mountain bikers, enthusiastic cyclists and sport students following a high-altitude training camp, compared with control subjects simply 'returning home'. From a strict scientific point of view, both studies suffer somewhat from not being strictly designated research projects with random group assignments, but rather opportunistically following ongoing behaviour of athletes, with lack of training data in one study (Peltonen et al., 2024). Nonetheless, what can be learned from these studies is that it might be possible to maintain an elevated Hbmass following high-altitude training. Whether this is related to an intervention-induced spike in circulating EPO remains unknown, because, in the study by Rønnestad et al. (2025), no attempts were made to delve into the physiological mechanisms, and, in contrast, no EPO spike was observed in the study by Peltonen et al. (2024), which could have been related to timing of the blood sampling. ## Heat exercise training

Acclimatization to a warm environment, whether combined with exercise training or not, has been known for decades to increase sweat rates and expand plasma volume, which are also associated with an increased exercise performance in such environments. In addition, daily 45-90 min exercise-training sessions performed for 10-14 days in a warm environment, but while living in a thermoneutral environment (i.e. heat training), has also been shown to expand plasma volume (Périard et al., 2021). It was while performing such a 14-day-long heat-training study (Keiser et al., 2015) that we came to speculate whether the heat-training-induced increase in plasma volume, and the consequent lowering of the haematocrit, could stimulate the production of red blood cells through the proposed 'critmeter' function (Dunn et al., 2007) of the kidney (Fig. 1). We reasoned that the previously performed heat-training studies lasting

10-14 days would be too short to increase red blood cell volume/Hbmass, because in previous studies where we had injected supraphysiological concentrations of recombinant EPO (Robach et al., 2021) or used altitude exposure (Siebenmann et al., 2015), a Hbmass response had first been observed after 2 and 3 weeks, respectively. In the first performed heat-training study with the intent to expand Hbmass (Oberholzer et al., 2019), we therefore opted for a 5-week intervention period and used moderately fit individuals, rather than elite athletes, with the rationale still to allow for adaptations. With five weekly training sessions, each lasting 60 min and performed at 60% of ˙ V O2max, we observed an increase in Hbmass that did not alter exercise performance, probably related to an underpowered study. As mentioned above, the variation associated with Hbmass assessment is typically lower than the variation for exercise performance testing. In terms of physiological mechanisms for the Hbmass expansion, not much was learned from this first study, because most analysis of blood volume-regulating hormones remained unchanged, which might have been related to missing a potential alteration owing to a narrow/limited sampling window.

As a next step, we tested whether a similar protocol, i.e. five weekly heat-training sessions, each lasting 45 min and performed at a rate of perceived exertion of ∼ 13/20 ('somewhat hard' on the Borg scale), for 5 weeks, but performed in actively trained cyclists, would have similar effects on Hbmass. Although Hbmass was increased by 46 g, the ˙ V O2max, lactate threshold and 15 min time-trial all-out exercise performance remained statistically unchanged (Rønnestad et al., 2021). There were, however, small to intermediate effect sizes favouring heat exercise training for lactate threshold, gross economy (in a fatigued state) and 15 min mean power.

The next step for us was to test whether such a protocol could be used to increase Hbmass andexercise performance in highly fit individuals, hence competing Norwegian cross-country skiers where enrolled into yet another study, which revealed a 30 g greater Hbmass, but, in contrast, no group differences were observed for changes in work economy, running velocity, ˙ V O2max or 15-min running distance (Rønnestad et al., 2022b). Thus, in initial heat exercise training studies, we could typically detect an increase in Hbmass, but with only very limited effects on exercise performance parameters. However, by increasing subject numbers, we were able to induce an increase Hbmass and in exercise performance, in trained individuals (Lundby et al., 2023; Rønnestad et al., 2022c).

These are obviously great study limitations, and it might therefore be questioned whether heat training might also be effective in regions of the world where ambient temperatures are higher than in Scandinavia, and if performed by other research teams.

Despite initial attempts to unravel the underlying mechanisms facilitating the expansion of Hbmass with heat training, thus far these have been in vain, and we can only speculate that the expansion might be related to our initial hypothesis (Keiser et al., 2015), i.e. secondary to an expanded plasma volume and according drop in haematocrit, as also outlined elsewhere (Montero &amp; Lundby, 2018). However, this might be entirely naïve, and other mechanisms might well be involved (Fig. 1), including: (pathway 1) a greater blood volume, allowing for a greater blood perfusion of skin during heat exposure, in turn providing a greater cooling effect, without compromising blood pressure; (pathway 2) a decrease in kidney blood flow during exercise in a warm environment, as blood flow is directed towards skin to provide cooling, whereby the decrease in kidney blood flow could result in local kidney hypoxia and thus provide a signal for augmented EPO synthesis; (pathway 3) a heat-training-induced reduction in plasma volume, decreasing central venous pressure, which has been shown to facilitate EPO synthesis (Montero et al., 2016); and/or (pathway 4) heat-training-induced plasma volume contraction, causing upregulation of sodium reabsorption and augmented O2 use, which results in local hypoxia in the kidney and thereby EPO synthesis.

Heat training has quickly become highly popular amongst athletes, as seen on YouTube, with producers of gadgets that might be more or less useful for monitoring different parameters associated with heat training. Based on these and looking back, the word 'training' in the context of heat training seems to cause confusion, because some advocators of heat training have proposed that the training during heat exposure should be hard and even of the interval type of intensities. Our take on this is that the 'training' or, perhaps better, 'physical activity' is a simple and highly effective way of increasing core temperature to a greater extent than if simply being exposed passively to heat (i.e. without exercise), but also that not much exercise intensity is needed in a warm environment or in an environment with poor cooling ability, in order to drive core temperature, hence sweat rate, up. In our practical experience (inclusing Pro and World Tour cyclists and Olympic calibre runners and skiers), heat training should be conducted at low enough exercise intensities to increase core temperature, but not at intensities that are normally considered as training (for athletes), with the intent not to interfere with the normal training regimes of athletes. The intent, in our opinion, is to increase core temperature (by whatever means) to facilitate an elevated sweat rate. The acute loss of sweat is, we speculate, what acutely lowers plasma volume, which ultimately might drive the chronic expansion of plasma volume and increase in Hbmass. Thus, an easy manner to determine whether a heat-training session has been successfully performed or not is thus simply by weighing the athlete before and after each heat-training session and note the (mainly) fluid loss. We recommend aiming at an initial 1 kg loss (a little less for small individuals, a little more for bigger individuals) for each session and adjustment according to athlete feedback from there. For this, no app or special sensor/watch is needed.

## Altitude exposure combined with heat training?

From a theoretical standpoint, the fact that exposure to either altitude or heat generally leads to an increase in Hbmass suggests that combining the two stressors might have a synergistic effect on Hbmass. This concept is currently gaining interest in elite sports. However, given that the two stressors have opposing effects on plasma volume (an increase with heat exposure vs . a decrease at altitude), the existence of a synergistic effect has yet to be established, as suggested by a recent review (Gibson et al., 2017). To date, there is no compelling evidence demonstrating an advantage from combining heat and hypoxia to boost Hbmass and/or performance, although we have practical knowledge from ProTour cyclists that such an approach is feasible and that the outcome is perceived as 'rather positive' . This question deserves further investigation.

## Conclusion

High-altitude training remains a popular training strategy to implement in the preparation of elite endurance athletes for major events. Both the haematological and performance benefits from such an approach are highly variable, and its reproducibility is questionable. In contrast, it seems that the haematological adaptations associated with heat training are far more robust, but also that the performance associated gains are small. Thus, heat training should perhaps not be implemented before all other steps involved in elite training are in place and, importantly, heat training should not be performed in such a manner that it might limit other aspects of the life of elite althletes.
